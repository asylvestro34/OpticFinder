Cable types, Ethernet speeds, and typical lengths.

A high-level cabling guide, independent of the devices available in the planner. Ethernet runs over both copper and fiber. Cable type alone does not determine the maximum speed or length; the Ethernet standard and installed channel must also meet the requirements.

Copper Ethernet: Cat5e to Cat8

For ordinary four-pair twisted-pair cabling, 100 m is the usual full-channel limit. That typically means up to 90 m of fixed cabling plus 10 m of patch leads.

* Existing cabling needs application-specific qualification, including interference between adjacent cables. Cat6 at 10G can be limited to shorter runs depending on the installation; 55 m is not guaranteed. Cat6A is the straightforward choice for a new 100 m 10G channel.

Cat7: An ISO/IEC category used in Class F channels, not a TIA category. Full Class F performance requires appropriate connectors and terminations; a cable advertised as Cat7 with ordinary RJ45 plugs does not establish a Class F channel.

Cat8: At 25G/40G, the limit is a 30 m channel, typically 24 m of fixed cable plus 6 m of patch leads. At 10G and below, a channel meeting the lower-speed application requirements can run to 100 m. TIA Cat8 and ISO Cat8.1 use RJ45-compatible connectors; ISO Cat8.2 uses a different connector system. Cat7 and Cat8 are shielded systems and require correct shield termination.

The planner includes Cat7 and RJ45-compatible Cat8/Cat8.1 as alternatives for its modeled 1G/2.5G/5G copper connections, subject to complete-channel qualification. Cat8.2 connectors are excluded. These cable choices do not add higher device speeds or qualify new transceivers.

These rows cover common BASE-T Ethernet applications, not every copper technology. Direct-attach cables, single-pair Ethernet, and specialist long-distance products have different limits.

Multimode fiber: OM1 to OM5

Multimode is commonly used within buildings and data centers. Higher speeds usually mean shorter runs. OM1 has a 62.5-micron core; OM2 through OM5 have a 50-micron core.

OM5 has the same lengths as OM4 for the applications above; it is not automatically longer-reaching. Other Ethernet standards can use the same fiber at different lengths. For example, 100GBASE-SR10 is different from the 100GBASE-SR4 row shown here.

Single-mode fiber: OS1 and OS2

Single-mode is commonly used for building-to-building, campus, and longer-distance links. Neither OS1 nor OS2 has one universal maximum Ethernet length. OS2 is commonly chosen for new longer-distance installations; check the actual cable's attenuation specification rather than relying on the grade name alone.

Installation basics

  • Measure the entire channel. Include patch leads, patch panels, and the actual cable route, not the straight-line distance.
  • Length is not the only limit. Connectors, splices, bends, damage, and interference can prevent an otherwise short-enough channel from meeting its Ethernet requirements.
  • Check the cable markings and test results. Jacket color alone does not reliably identify fiber grade or cable category.
  • Match the full application. Both ends must support the same Ethernet standard. Fiber count and connector arrangement also need to suit that application.

This alpha reference is simplified and may contain inaccuracies. It does not certify an installed channel or change the planner's device-specific connection rules. Management and stacking ports remain excluded from link planning.
